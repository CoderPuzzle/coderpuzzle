# Solutions — Super Palindromes

## Enumerate the palindromic roots

A super-palindrome is fixed by its root: the root must itself be a
palindrome, so candidates come from the roots, never from the values in
range. A palindrome is decided by its first half — mirror the half and the
whole number follows — so every root of up to nine digits is generated by
walking each width's halves and appending the reverse of the half's own
head. Nine digits of root suffice because `right` is below `10¹⁸` and the
root of anything below `10¹⁸` is below `10⁹`. Each generated root is
squared, and the square counts exactly when it is itself a palindrome and
lands in `[left, right]`.

Roots enumerated by width, then by half, ascend numerically — every
`k`-digit root is smaller than every `(k+1)`-digit one, and a larger half
mirrors to a larger root — so the squares ascend with them. The scan
therefore runs from the smallest root upward and stops at the first square
above `right`: nothing later can re-enter the range, and a tiny range like
`[1, 2]` costs two roots of work. Even at the full `10¹⁸` ceiling the scan
visits only the 109,998 palindromic roots of up to nine digits, and the
survivors number just 70 — `1, 4, 9, 121, 484, 10201, …` — which is also
why no precomputed list is needed: the construction is cheaper to run than
a table is to carry.

Every value in play fits a signed 64-bit integer: roots stay below `10⁹`
and the widest product is `999,999,999² < 10¹⁸`, an order of magnitude
inside int64's `9.22 × 10¹⁸` ceiling — so Java, C++, Go, and Rust carry
the roots, squares, and both parsed bounds in `long` / `long long` /
`int64` / `i64` (Python's integers are unbounded anyway). JavaScript and
TypeScript cannot follow: `Number` holds integers exactly only through
`2⁵³ ≈ 9 × 10¹⁵`, and both the bounds and the squares tower past it —
those two keep the roots, squares, and bounds in `BigInt`, with only the
half counter and the count as ordinary numbers.

**Complexity:** `O(R)` time, `O(1)` space, where `R = 109,998` is the
number of palindromic roots of up to nine digits — the scan's full width
at the `10¹⁸` ceiling.
